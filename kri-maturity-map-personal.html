Operational risk · Key risk indicators

The KRI maturity map

Hitesh Patel · Enterprise risk management, global asset management

Most KRI programs are judged on how many indicators they report. That is the wrong measure. The constraint is trust: a number assembled by hand, weeks after the period closed, from a source only the preparer can explain, cannot support a decision in the moment it is needed. This map sets out five levels, ending where the indicator set is integrated with KPIs and KCIs, calibrated from event history, produced automatically from systems of record, and open to the second line at source rather than by request.

Figure 1 — Five levels of KRI maturity

1Initial
Anecdotal
Numbers are quoted in meetings, but nothing is defined as an indicator of a named risk.
  • No indicator library; metrics chosen by whoever is presenting
  • No stated link to a risk in the taxonomy
  • No thresholds, so no such thing as a breach
  • Reporting is retrospective commentary
What the business feels: numbers in a slide pack.
2Repeatable
Manual and fragmented
A KRI list exists. It is assembled by hand, from local extracts, after the fact.
  • Indicators named and owned, but defined inconsistently
  • Data pulled manually from spreadsheets and local extracts
  • Thresholds set by judgment, rarely revisited
  • Reporting is monthly at best, and always backward-looking
What the business feels: a monthly data-gathering exercise.
3Defined
Calibrated
One library, one definition per indicator, and thresholds that mean something.
  • Documented definition, calculation, owner, and source per indicator
  • Each KRI mapped to a risk in the taxonomy
  • Thresholds tied to risk appetite, with defined escalation
  • Breach triggers a documented response, not a discussion
What the business feels: a defined control on the risk profile.
4Managed
Integrated and data-driven
Indicators sit alongside performance and control measures, and thresholds are set from evidence.
  • KRIs, KPIs, and KCIs read together against the same process
  • Thresholds calibrated from loss, incident, and breach history
  • Source systems named; lineage documented and reviewed
  • Predictive value tested; indicators that never move are retired
What the business feels: an early-warning system.
5Optimized
Automated and transparent
Indicators are produced straight from systems of record, and the second line can see the same data without asking.
  • Straight-through production from systems of record; no manual assembly
  • Second line self-serves the underlying data and the lineage
  • Breach detection and escalation are automatic and timestamped
  • Indicator set is reviewed continuously against emerging exposure
What the business feels: a live view they trust.

The shift that defines the end state

Levels 1 to 3 make indicators consistent. Level 4 makes them meaningful. Level 5 makes them trustworthy without a preparer standing behind them — produced automatically, and open to the second line at source.

Figure 2 — Reported metrics against operating signal

Reported metrics

  • Assembled by hand, weeks after the period closes
  • Provenance known only to the preparer, and verified by request
  • Thresholds set by judgment and rarely revisited
  • The pack is the outcome
→

Operating signal

  • Produced straight from systems of record, continuously
  • Second line sees the source and the lineage without asking
  • Thresholds calibrated from event history and back-tested
  • Read alongside KPIs and KCIs; the response is the outcome

Level 5: the four attributes of the end state

Four attributes define the end state. They are cumulative, and the order matters: automation built on uncalibrated thresholds and undocumented lineage produces bad signal faster, and with more authority. What each attribute buys, and what it costs, is set out below.

Figure 4 — The four end-state attributes

Attribute A

Integrated across KPI, KCI, and KRI

What good looks like
Performance, control, and risk indicators are read together against the same process, on the same cycle, in one view. Divergence between them is the signal: throughput rising while control effectiveness falls is a finding, not a coincidence.
What it requires
A common process taxonomy across the three measure types, shared ownership between the business and the second line, and one reporting cycle rather than three.
What it costs
Forces a single owner for a view that three functions currently produce separately. That negotiation, not the tooling, is the hard part.
Becomes real at Level 4
Attribute B

Data-driven thresholds

What good looks like
Thresholds are set from loss, incident, and breach history rather than from judgment, and are back-tested against events that actually occurred. Indicators that never move, or that move only after the event, are retired.
What it requires
Sufficient event history, a defined recalibration cycle, and the discipline to remove indicators that fail the test.
What it costs
Requires accepting that some long-standing indicators have no predictive value, and that removing them will be read by some as reducing coverage.
Becomes real at Level 4
Attribute C

Transparent data sources to the second line

What good looks like
The second line can see the source system, the calculation, and the lineage for any indicator without requesting an extract. Challenge moves from asking where the number came from to asking what it means.
What it requires
Documented and tested lineage, read access to the underlying data, and data quality controls on the feed rather than on the finished report.
What it costs
Exposes source data quality that manual preparation used to smooth over. Expect an initial rise in apparent issues.
Becomes real at Level 5
Attribute D

Fully automated production

What good looks like
Indicators are produced straight from systems of record with no manual assembly, breaches are detected and escalated automatically, and the timestamp is the evidence.
What it requires
Controlled feeds, exception handling, and monitoring of the pipeline itself.
What it costs
The pipeline becomes a control that must be assessed. Automation removes the manual check that used to catch a bad feed, so that check has to be rebuilt in the data layer.
Becomes real at Level 5
Data qualityThe feed becomes the control. Completeness, timeliness, and accuracy checks sit on the pipeline, with breaks routed to a named owner rather than absorbed silently.
Indicator lifecycleEvery indicator has a review date. Additions require a stated risk linkage; retirement requires evidence that predictive value was tested and failed.
Automation does not replace judgmentAn automatic breach still requires human assessment of cause and response. The system detects and escalates; it does not conclude.

What has to be true to move up

Each level depends on the one below it. The most common failure I see is automating production before definitions, thresholds, and lineage are settled. The output arrives faster, looks more official, and is no more reliable than the manual version it replaced.

Figure 5 — Entry conditions for each level

1 → 2
  • Indicator list assembled, with a named owner for each
  • A single reporting cycle agreed
  • Source of each number identified, even if manual
2 → 3
  • Documented definition and calculation per indicator
  • Each KRI mapped to a risk in the taxonomy
  • Thresholds tied to appetite, with escalation paths defined
3 → 4
  • KRI, KPI, and KCI aligned to one process taxonomy
  • Enough event history to calibrate and back-test thresholds
  • Lineage documented for each indicator's source
4 → 5
  • Read access and controlled feeds from systems of record
  • Data quality controls sitting on the feed, not the report
  • Automation and its exception handling accepted as controls to be assessed

Source: Author's framework, drawing on common key risk indicator and operational risk practice and concepts consistent with COSO ERM and ISO 31000. It is not taken from a published standard. Level definitions, attributes, and entry conditions should be reconciled to a firm's own ERM policy, indicator library, and appetite methodology before use.
