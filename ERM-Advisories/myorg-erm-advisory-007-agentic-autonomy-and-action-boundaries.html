Agentic AI — autonomy limits and action boundaries

Autonomy is granted, never assumed. This advisory sets out what Enterprise Risk Management expects where AI is permitted to take actions rather than produce drafts.

Section 1

Purpose

Agentic systems differ from assistants in one respect that matters for control design: they act. They call tools, write to systems, send communications, and decompose goals into steps that were not individually approved. The gap between an incorrect output and an incorrect action can be very short.

This advisory sets out what ERM expects of the boundaries placed around agentic capability where business processes are augmented with it. It should be read with ERM-ADV-AI-006 on identity and entitlements and ERM-ADV-AI-001 on human oversight.

Section 2

The control objective

A control exists to achieve an objective. For this control, the objective is:

Control Objective

To ensure that where an AI system is permitted to take action, the set of permissible actions, the maximum consequence of those actions, and the conditions requiring human authorization are defined in advance and enforced by the system rather than by instruction.

A boundary that exists only as a sentence in a prompt is not an enforced boundary. Where a submission describes autonomy limits, ERM will ask which component refuses the action when the limit is reached.

Section 3

Principles

Autonomy is granted explicitly

The default position is that an AI system drafts and a human acts. Any departure from that is a specific, recorded grant covering defined action types within defined limits.

Reversibility determines the boundary

Actions that can be undone cheaply may be automated at lower cost of control. Actions that are irreversible, externally visible, or financially material require authorization regardless of confidence in the system.

Blast radius must be bounded

Value, volume, rate, and scope limits contain the consequence of an error or a compromise. Without them, a single fault can propagate at machine speed before anyone notices.

Chained actions compound

Multi-step and multi-agent workflows can produce emergent sequences no one specified. Assess the workflow's worst credible end state, not the risk of each step in isolation.

Stop must always be available

There must be a way to halt the system, a defined safe state to halt into, and a tested runbook covering who does it and what happens to work in flight.

Section 5

Where autonomous action should not be permitted

Autonomy should not be granted, and human authorization should be required for every action, where any of the following apply:

Value movementPayments, transfers, trading, or any action with direct financial effect.
External communicationMessages sent to clients, counterparties, regulators, or the public.
System or access changeConfiguration, permission, or infrastructure changes, including to the agent's own environment.
Self-directed decompositionThe agent determines its own sub-goals or spawns further agents without bounded scope.
ERM expectation: in these cases, the agent proposes and a human disposes. Where a business case requires autonomy in one of these areas, it is escalated for governance decision with compensating controls — hard limits, dual authorization, real-time monitoring, and a tested stop — explicitly set out.
Section 6

Roles & responsibilities

First line

Use-case owners

Define and enforce the action boundaries, test failure scenarios, and maintain the stop runbook.

Governance

AI governance

Challenge autonomy grants at intake and confirm enforcement sits outside the model.

Second line

Enterprise risk management

Independently test boundary enforcement and stop capability on a risk basis.

Third line

Internal audit

Provide independent assurance over the framework and selected use-cases.
