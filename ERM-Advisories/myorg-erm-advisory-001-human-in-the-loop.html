“Human in the loop” as a control — expectations and minimum standards

Naming a control is not the same as designing one. This advisory sets out what Enterprise Risk Management expects for Human in the Loop (HITL) to be recognized as an effective control in AI use-cases.

Section 1

Purpose

AI use-cases submitted for governance review increasingly cite “Human in the Loop” (HITL) as a key mitigating control. ERM supports human oversight of AI — but naming a control is not the same as designing one.

This advisory clarifies what ERM expects for HITL to be recognized as an effective control, so that use-case owners can design it properly and reviewers can assess it consistently.

Section 2

The control objective

A control exists to achieve an objective. For HITL, the objective is:

Control Objective

To ensure that erroneous, incomplete, biased, non-compliant, or otherwise inappropriate AI-generated outputs are identified and corrected by a competent human before they result in an action, decision, or communication that could harm clients, the firm, employees, or markets.

If a proposed HITL arrangement cannot plausibly achieve this objective in practice, it is not a control — it is a label. Submissions citing HITL must state the control objective in the context of the specific use-case: what could go wrong with the AI output, and what the human review is expected to catch.

Section 3

Principles

Oversight must be meaningful, not nominal

The reviewer must genuinely engage with the output, not merely acknowledge it. A workflow in which a person clicks “approve” on hundreds of AI outputs per day does not meet the control objective, regardless of what the process document says.

HITL is vulnerable to human-factor risks

Automation bias, review fatigue, time pressure, and skill atrophy all degrade HITL effectiveness over time. Control design must anticipate these — not assume a diligent reviewer in perpetuity.

HITL is one layer, not a substitute

Human review does not replace model validation, input controls, output guardrails, monitoring, or access management. Higher-risk use-cases should not lean on HITL as the sole or primary mitigant.

Proportionality applies

The rigor of HITL should scale with the use-case's risk rating, the consequence of an undetected error, and the degree of autonomy the AI is given.

Accountability stays with the human

Where HITL is the control, the reviewer — and ultimately the use-case owner — is accountable for the output that leaves the process. “The AI produced it” is not a defense.

Section 5

When HITL alone is not sufficient

HITL should not be presented as the primary control where any of the following apply:

Volume or speedOutput volume or speed makes genuine review impracticable.
AutonomyThe AI acts autonomously — executing actions rather than drafting for review.
DetectabilityErrors are difficult for a human to detect by inspection (e.g., subtle numerical or data-lineage errors).
Risk ratingThe use-case is rated high risk under the firm's AI risk framework.
ERM expectation: in these cases, complementary controls are required — pre-deployment validation, automated guardrails, sampling-based quality assurance, and continuous monitoring — with HITL as a supporting layer.
Section 6

Roles & responsibilities

First line

Use-case owners

Design HITL to these standards, operate it, retain evidence, and monitor its ongoing effectiveness.

Governance

AI governance

Assess HITL design against this advisory at intake; challenge submissions where HITL is asserted without substance.

Second line

Enterprise risk management

Provide independent challenge, test control design and operation on a risk basis, and maintain this advisory.

Third line

Internal audit

Provide independent assurance over the framework and selected use-cases.
